Interacting particles · critical phenomena

Activated random walk

In the 1980s, Per Bak, Chao Tang, and Kurt Wiesenfeld coined and popularized self-organized criticality (SOC), a phenomenon they observed in systems like avalanches and forest fires. Energy slowly builds and is released in shocks whose sizes follow a statistical power law. Their mathematical models include the abelian sandpile, a precursor to activated random walk (ARW).

Simulations suggest that ARW has a robust critical limiting state, but a mathematical description of exactly how SOC arises is still missing. The two-dimensional case is especially compelling; one dimension, trees, and mean field provide more tractable laboratories that are not yet fully solved.

02

Symbolic dynamics · pattern avoidance

Shifts of finite type & patterns

Fix a family of forbidden patterns on a transitive graph: some colorings of some finite subgraphs. Consider an iid coloring on the full graph, or a finite subset, conditioned to avoid every forbidden pattern. What does a typical configuration look like, and what statistical properties does it have?

Many interesting questions arise already in one dimension. The entropy after forbidding a single binary word is described by its autocorrelation polynomial. In higher dimensions, computing entropy or establishing mixing is much harder; another natural problem is to understand the density of a fixed allowable pattern.

03

Random matrices · statistical inference

Random matrices

A recurring theme in the study of large finite graphs is to describe hidden geometric features. A random graph ensemble has geometry if it can be roughly embedded in a low-dimensional metric space. For Wishart matrices—random graphs embedded in a sphere—one can characterize a transition between detectable geometry and apparent noise.

Random intersection graphs share essentially the same transition. This suggests a universal phenomenon: can one characterize a broad class of random graphs that lose geometry at the same threshold?

Combinatorial stochastic processes

I am often drawn to questions about random combinatorial objects: Markov chains, fragmentation/coagulation processes, renewal processes, random number theory, and random algorithms. Proving limit theorems, describing stationary configurations or evolutions, or studying mean-field behavior often leads to cutting-edge ideas and unsolved problems.

Inverse problems · perturbations · interacting paths

Random walks

Random walks recur throughout my work in several different guises. Given only a trace, can one recover where a walk began? What happens when ordinary random steps alternate with a fixed permutation of the vertices? How do systems of walkers collide, coalesce, or annihilate?

On a regular tree, a permuted random walk cannot be slowed down. In source detection, even the apparently basic problem on ℤd remains difficult. The occupation map at right hides one piece of information on purpose—make a guess before clicking.
